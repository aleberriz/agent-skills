# 03 — Choosing words that work

Word choice is where most prose silently fails. The structure can be sound and the argument can be right, but if the words are abstract, hedged, jargony, or hyped, the reader disengages.

## Concrete over abstract

Prefer words that name things you could see, count, or stub your toe on. Concrete language gives the reader something to hold; abstract language gives them fog.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "improve operational efficiency" | "cut deploy time from 40 minutes to 12" |
| "stakeholder feedback" | "what three customers told sales last week" |
| "substantial market opportunity" | "$42M of revenue we are not yet capturing" |
| "leverage synergies" | "share the same warehouse pipeline" |

Concrete language also forces you to *check* whether you actually know the specific. If you cannot make a sentence concrete, the underlying thinking may not be ready.

## Old, short words over Latinate ones

English has two large vocabularies layered on top of each other: a core of short, blunt words (largely Anglo-Saxon in origin) and a wider register of longer, more formal words (largely Latin and French in origin). Both are valid English. The shorter set is almost always clearer for business prose.

| Latinate | Short |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help |
| demonstrate | show |
| commence | start |
| terminate | end |
| endeavor | try |
| approximately | about |
| in the event that | if |
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |

Use the longer word only when it carries a specific meaning the shorter one cannot. "Conclude" is more than "end"; keep it when the difference matters. Otherwise, choose the shorter form.

## Verbs do the work

Strong verbs carry sentences. Weak verbs ("is," "has," "does") force you to load adjectives and nouns to compensate.

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| "There is a need for the team to address…" | "The team needs to address…" |
| "She is in agreement with the proposal." | "She agrees with the proposal." |
| "The system has the ability to scale." | "The system scales." |
| "He made the decision to leave." | "He decided to leave." |

Watch for nouns that are really verbs in disguise (often ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*). "Make a decision" is a verb pretending to be a noun phrase. Convert: "decide."

## Avoid jargon — except between specialists

Jargon serves two purposes. It is **shorthand** between people who already share a vocabulary, and it is a **tribal marker** that signals "I belong to this group." The first is useful in narrow contexts; the second is what tanks business prose.

A practical rule:

> Jargon is fine between consenting specialists.

Two analysts comparing topics in a semantic layer can use *grain*, *primary key*, and *spine* fluently. The same words in a memo to an executive who has not built one will obscure the point. When in doubt, use the everyday word and gloss the specialist term in parentheses on first use.

The deeper problem with jargon is that it lets the writer *appear* to know something without actually committing to a claim. "We are pursuing operational excellence" sounds like a strategy; it is not one. Force yourself to write what you would say to a smart friend over coffee, and the jargon usually falls out on its own.

## Avoid euphemism that obscures

Euphemisms are words that soften an uncomfortable truth. In personal life they have a place — telling a child their painting is "interesting" is kinder than telling them the truth. In business writing they cost you trust.

| Euphemism | Plain |
|---|---|
| "right-sizing" | "layoffs" |
| "challenges" | "problems" |
| "headwinds" | "we missed our number because…" |
| "in the process of being addressed" | "not done yet" |

If you cannot say the plain version, you do not yet know what you mean.

## Avoid hype that exaggerates

The mirror image of euphemism is overhype. "Revolutionary," "groundbreaking," "world-class," "best-in-class," "transformative." Each of these words can be earned, but most uses do not earn them. Readers learn to discount them, and the writer who uses them for ordinary improvements has nothing left to say when something is genuinely remarkable.

A better default: state the specific that would justify the strong word, and let the reader supply the adjective.

> Not: "We achieved revolutionary improvements in onboarding."
> But: "Onboarding now takes 3 minutes, down from 11."

## Steelman the counter-argument

When the piece is persuasive — a recommendation, a proposal, a memo arguing against a course of action — express the strongest version of the opposing view before you respond to it. This:

- forces you to actually understand the counter-argument;
- makes your response more credible to readers who hold that view;
- prevents the strawman trap of arguing with a weaker version of the case than the one your real opponents hold.

A useful structure:

> "The case against doing X is strong: A, B, and C. Despite these, we should still do X, because…"

## Inclusivity

Default to gender-neutral phrasing unless gender is specifically the topic.

- Use the **singular *they*** when the actor is unknown or generic.
- Pluralize when convenient: "engineers configure their tools" beats "an engineer configures his tools."
- Refer to people by their **role or work**, not by spousal or family relationships, unless that relationship is the point.
- Be wary of vocabulary that has acquired gendered associations even though the words themselves are neutral. (Job postings asking for someone "independent and self-reliant" are read as more masculine-coded than postings asking for someone "dependable and reliable" — even though both are perfectly applicable to anyone.)

## Quality checks for word choice

Run these once you have a complete draft:

- [ ] Are the verbs vivid? Could any *be*-verb sentence be rewritten with a stronger verb?
- [ ] Is there jargon a non-specialist would not parse? Replace or gloss it.
- [ ] Are there hedges, intensifiers, or filler phrases that can be cut?
- [ ] Are claims of *greatness* or *transformation* earned by specifics elsewhere in the piece?
- [ ] Is the language inclusive by default?
