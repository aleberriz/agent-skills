# 04 — Writing clear and engaging sentences

Sentences are where word choice meets rhythm. Even a draft with strong words and good structure can read poorly if the sentences are heavy, monotonous, or miscalibrated.

## Tools, not rules

Treat grammar advice as **tools, not rules**. The point of every guideline below is to make a sentence do its job. When breaking a guideline serves the reader better, break it.

That said, a small set of defaults make most sentences better. Apply them by default, and notice when you are deviating.

## Active voice as the default

The active voice puts the actor at the start of the sentence and the action at its core.

> "The team shipped the migration on Friday."

The passive voice moves the actor to the end or omits it entirely.

> "The migration was shipped on Friday."
> "The migration was shipped on Friday by the team."

Active voice is shorter, clearer, and usually more honest, because it forces you to name who did what. Use the passive only when:

- The actor is genuinely unknown ("The package was left at the door").
- The actor is irrelevant to the point ("Glass is recycled in this region").
- The action is the focus and the actor would distract ("The bug was introduced in the November release").
- You are deliberately softening a difficult message — but be honest with yourself when this is the reason.

## Short sentences, used freely

Long sentences in business prose are almost always two or three short sentences fused with commas, semicolons, and conjunctions. Full stops are free; use them.

> "Although we had originally planned to ship the feature in Q1, due to scope changes that emerged in the second sprint, the team has now agreed to push the release to Q2, which will give us time to address the open performance issues that surfaced during load testing."

becomes

> "We had originally planned to ship the feature in Q1. Scope grew in the second sprint, and we surfaced performance issues during load testing. The team has now agreed to push the release to Q2."

Three short sentences, each carrying one idea, with the same information.

A useful diagnostic: read your draft aloud (or imagine reading it). Anywhere your breath runs out, consider a full stop.

## Sentence weighting — punchline at the end

The end of a sentence is its strongest position. Put the most important word, name, or number there.

> Weaker: "The biggest risk is regulatory uncertainty in our planning."
> Stronger: "The biggest risk in our planning is regulatory uncertainty."

The same applies to paragraphs: open with the topic sentence, end with the heavy word. Jokes work this way; so do arguments.

## Vary sentence length deliberately

A run of identically short sentences feels staccato. A run of identically long sentences feels like wading. The cure is variation: most sentences short, with longer ones used when the content earns the length, and the very-short sentence reserved for emphasis.

> "We launched on Tuesday. Adoption was slow. We re-targeted the onboarding email at the end of the week, and within 48 hours signups doubled. The lesson was simple. Pick the right channel."

Notice how the four-word sentence near the end ("The lesson was simple.") lands harder than it would in the middle of a long paragraph.

## Metaphors — fresh, fit, and few

Metaphor is one of the most powerful tools in business writing because it lets you carry a complicated idea inside a familiar image. The pitfalls are equally well-known.

- **Avoid clichés**. "Move the needle," "boil the ocean," "shift the paradigm" — once a metaphor becomes a phrase, it stops creating an image and starts creating noise.
- **Keep metaphors fresh**. The first time you reach for a metaphor in a piece, ask whether you have read it elsewhere this month. If yes, find another image.
- **Match the metaphor to the audience**. Sports metaphors, military metaphors, and US-specific cultural references all break down quickly with a global audience.
- **Watch the implications**. Some metaphors carry baggage you do not want. The "war on cancer" example is well known: cast as a fight, the disease implies that patients who die "did not fight hard enough." Choose images that do not carry such freight.
- **Do not pile them up**. One running metaphor per piece is usually plenty. Mixed metaphors ("we'll boil the ocean and then run with the ball") confuse and amuse, in that order.

When a complex idea resists explanation, ask: *what familiar thing is this most like?* The answer is often a usable metaphor: a factory, a kitchen, a transit map, a thermostat.

## Imagery beyond metaphor

Concrete imagery — a specific anecdote, a striking number, a vivid example — is to business prose what evidence is to argument. A single well-chosen detail does more than a paragraph of generalization. The "64 elephants on a ballerina's toe" trick (used to describe gigapascals of pressure) is memorable precisely because it builds an image you cannot easily forget.

When you have a striking number, push it forward. When you have an anecdote that captures the broader pattern, lead with it.

## Rhythm — prose as music

Good prose has a rhythm even when no one notices it. Bad prose stumbles even when readers cannot say why.

- **Read aloud.** This is the single most reliable test. Anything that trips your tongue trips the reader's eye.
- **Mind the cadence.** A sentence has a rhythm; a paragraph has a rhythm; a piece has a rhythm. Variation in sentence length is rhythm in slow motion.
- **Avoid accidental rhymes and repeated sounds**. They draw attention away from the meaning. ("The strategy emphasizes the legacy of energy efficiency" — the *-y* repetition wins, the meaning loses.)

## Inclusive sentence-level habits

Several inclusivity points carry over from word choice; a few are sentence-level.

- **Pluralize** when it lets you avoid pronoun gymnastics: "engineers configure their tools" sidesteps "his/her" entirely.
- **Refer to people by what they do**, not by who they are married to, when the relationship is not the point.
- **Be alert to default-male framing** ("the analyst… he"). Either pluralize or use the singular *they*.

## Common sentence-level anti-patterns

Cut on sight:

- **Throat-clearing openers**: "It is important to note that…", "It can be argued that…", "In today's fast-paced world…".
- **Stacked qualifiers**: "It might be the case that there could potentially be…". Pick one hedge or none.
- **Double negatives** that are not deliberate: "not unlikely" is just "likely" — and likely beats not unlikely.
- **Empty intensifiers**: very, really, truly, literally, clearly, basically, just. Almost all delete cleanly.
- **Wordy connectives**: "in order to" → "to"; "due to the fact that" → "because"; "in spite of the fact that" → "although"; "at this point in time" → "now."
- **Redundant pairs**: "completely eliminate," "free gift," "advance planning," "future plans." Cut the duplicate word.

## Quality checks for sentences

- [ ] Most sentences are active.
- [ ] No sentence runs longer than the reader can hold in one breath without good reason.
- [ ] Each sentence ends on its strongest word.
- [ ] Metaphors are fresh, fit, and used sparingly.
- [ ] Read aloud, the piece flows; nothing trips.
