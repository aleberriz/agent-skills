# 01 — Planning for impact

Planning is the work of deciding *what* you are writing and *for whom*, before you decide *how* to write it. Skipping this step is the most common cause of muddled drafts, scope creep, and rework.

## The four planning questions

Answer each in a single sentence before drafting. If you cannot, the piece is not ready to be written.

| Question | Probe |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | What change should this writing produce in the reader? A decision, an action, an understanding, an alignment? |
| **Audience** | Who reads this? What do they already know? What will make them push back? |
| **Message** | If the reader keeps one sentence, what is it? |
| **Structure** | What is the minimum sequence of points that gets them from where they are to that one sentence? |

When the agent is writing on a user's behalf, surface any of these that are unclear. A short clarifying question early is cheaper than a rewrite later.

## Sketching before drafting

For anything longer than a few paragraphs, sketch a structure before writing prose. Two methods that work well:

- **Linear outline.** A bulleted list of points in the order you intend to make them. One paragraph per bullet.
- **Idea map.** Topic in the middle of the page, themes radiating out, links between related ideas. Useful when you do not yet know the right order, or when the topic has many sub-themes.

The sketch becomes the candidate executive summary or introduction once the piece is drafted. It also tells you when a point is missing (jump from A to D with no B or C) or when one is in the wrong place.

## The map and the journey

Treat your sketch as the *map*. Treat the actual writing as the *journey*. The map is a hypothesis about how the journey should go; the journey often discovers a better path.

When the two diverge, you have a question to answer:

- Was the **map wrong**? The new path you found is better; update the outline and continue.
- Was the **driving wrong**? You wandered. Cut the detour, return to the map.

Either answer is fine. The mistake is to ignore the divergence and ship a piece whose stated structure no longer matches its actual content.

## Picking a register and length

The same content can be a one-line Slack reply, a paragraph email, a one-page memo, or a multi-section report. Length is a function of audience and purpose, not of how much the writer knows.

A useful test: imagine the reader skimming on a phone with two minutes to spare. What is the smallest version of this piece that still serves the purpose? Anything beyond that is a candidate for an appendix or a follow-up.

## Productivity habits that compound

These habits matter less than the question of *whether* you write at all, but they help once you have committed to writing regularly.

- **Lower the activation energy.** Have the document open before you sit down. Have the outline visible. Eliminate the choose-a-tab moment.
- **Time-box.** A 25-minute focused block (Pomodoro-style) is enough to make progress on most pieces and short enough to start when motivation is low.
- **Start anywhere.** You do not have to write the introduction first. Start with the section you have the clearest view of and let the rest follow.
- **Get a draft, however ugly.** A complete bad draft is more workable than a beautifully phrased opening paragraph followed by nothing. Aim for breadth before depth.
- **Take real breaks.** Walking, in particular, helps unstick problems. Solutions often surface a few minutes after stepping away.
- **Externalize when stuck.** Explaining the piece out loud to another person, or writing the explanation as if you were emailing it to a friend, almost always reveals the right framing.
- **Use a hard deadline.** "By Thursday EOD" focuses planning more than "this week" or "soon."

## Diagnosing writer's block

Writer's block is usually a thinking problem in disguise. When the words will not come, ask:

- Do I know my **audience**? If unclear, the cost of every sentence rises.
- Do I know my **message**? If you cannot say it in one sentence aloud, you cannot write it in many.
- Am I trying to **start at the start**? Skip to a section you can write and circle back.
- Am I missing **information**? If yes, stop writing and go gather it; you will write the section in 20 minutes once you have what you need.
- Am I trying to **be clever**? Drop the ambition; aim for clear and complete first.

## Outputs of the planning step

Before moving to drafting, the agent should be able to point at:

- A one-sentence **purpose, audience, message, structure** for the piece.
- A short **outline** (3–10 bullets for most documents).
- A working **title** that signals the purpose.

If any of these are missing, drafting is premature.
