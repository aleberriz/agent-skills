# 02 — Crafting your structure

Structure is the order and grouping of your points. Good structure makes a piece feel inevitable: each paragraph follows the last and prepares the next. Poor structure makes a piece feel like a list of things the writer happened to know.

## Paragraph as the unit of thought

The paragraph is the smallest unit that carries a complete idea. A useful rule:

> One paragraph, one point.

If a paragraph is making two points, split it. If a sentence wanders into a second idea, end it with a full stop and start a new one. Long paragraphs in business prose are almost always two or three short paragraphs that have been wrongly fused.

## Ordering the points

The right order depends on the kind of piece you are writing.

### Persuasive pieces — state the conclusion early

When you are arguing for a position, the reader needs to know your destination before they can evaluate the route. Open with the claim, then build the case.

A useful pattern within the body:

- **Smallest argument first**, biggest argument last. The piece feels like it gathers force as it progresses.
- The opposite (biggest first, then minor points) tends to deflate; the reader's attention falls as the stakes drop.

If you find yourself reluctantly persuaded by your own evidence — if your conclusion changed during writing — say so. A piece that visibly weighs the counter-argument is more persuasive than one that does not acknowledge it exists.

### Informational pieces — frontload the news

When you are reporting findings (an analysis, a release note, a status update), use the journalistic **inverted pyramid**:

- Lead paragraph: the headline finding.
- Next paragraph(s): the supporting evidence and context.
- Later paragraphs: caveats, methodology, edge cases.

The reader who stops after one paragraph still knows the most important thing.

### Comparative pieces — pick a thread

When you are comparing places, products, or scenarios, choose a single thread that runs through the piece (a question, a metric, a metaphor) and use the cases as illustrations of that thread. A piece that lists "in case A this happens; in case B that happens" with no thread becomes inventory, not analysis.

### Idea-driven pieces — signpost relentlessly

Abstract pieces — about strategy, about ideas, about why something matters — risk losing the reader at every paragraph. Compensate with explicit signposts: "First…", "But the bigger reason is…", "This matters because…". Signposting feels heavy-handed to the writer; it almost never feels heavy-handed to the reader.

## Openings

Three reliable opening moves:

- **Scene or anecdote.** A specific moment that frames the broader topic. Best for longer pieces.
- **Striking number.** A single fact that makes the reader want to know more. Best when the data is the point.
- **Problem statement.** A clear sentence about what is broken, missing, or at stake. Best for memos, proposals, and PR descriptions.

Whichever you pick, **the second sentence has to do work too**. If the opener is a scene, the second sentence should hint at why the scene matters. If it is a number, the second sentence should put it in context. If it is a problem statement, the second sentence should preview the response.

## Endings

Avoid two anti-patterns at the close:

- **The recap.** "In summary, we discussed X, Y and Z." If the piece is short enough that a recap helps, the piece is too long.
- **The whimper.** Trailing off into a generic closing thought ("There is much more to say…").

Better endings:

- **Forward-looking note.** What this opens up next; what the reader could do or watch for.
- **Return to the opening.** Pick up the image, anecdote, or number from the lead and resolve it.
- **Decisive sentence.** A short, weighty sentence that lands.

## Sourcing and evidence

Structure is also about whom the piece relies on. Conventions:

- **Name your sources.** Inline credit (a person, a paper, an organization) is honest and adds authority. Hidden sourcing weakens claims.
- **Prefer primary sources.** Going to the original document or dataset takes longer and almost always produces a stronger piece. You also catch context the secondary source had stripped out.
- **Apply polite skepticism.** Ask whether the source has an agenda. Most do; that does not disqualify them, but it should shape how you frame their evidence.
- **Triangulate when data is patchy.** Multiple weaker sources that agree are usually more persuasive than one strong source standing alone.
- **Caveat openly.** If a number is uncertain, say so. Hidden uncertainty is more damaging when it surfaces later than declared uncertainty would have been.

## Comparing fairly

When the structure of your argument depends on a comparison (across countries, products, periods), make the comparison apples-to-apples:

- Same time window.
- Same definition (real vs nominal, gross vs net, headline vs core).
- Same scope (all customers vs active customers).
- Same units, currency, or normalization.

If you cannot match exactly, **state the mismatch** and explain why the comparison still informs.

## Putting numbers in context

Numbers in structure (as distinct from charts, covered separately):

- A single number is rarely informative. Pair it with a comparison, a baseline, or a familiar analogy.
- Distinguish **relative from absolute**. "Risk doubled" can mean a tiny absolute change or a huge one.
- Distinguish a **point** from a **trend**. One month's data point can mislead; a multi-period trend is harder to fake.
- Be honest about the **frame**. Choosing a flattering start year or comparison group is the easiest way to mislead without lying. If the frame matters, justify it.

## Quality checks for structure

Run these before moving to language-level editing:

- [ ] Each paragraph carries one point.
- [ ] The order moves from where the reader is to where you want them to be.
- [ ] Persuasive pieces state their conclusion early; informational pieces lead with the news.
- [ ] Each comparison uses comparable units and time windows.
- [ ] Sources are named where credibility matters.
- [ ] The opening earns the reader's next paragraph; the close points forward.
