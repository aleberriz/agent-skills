# 06 — Editing your work

Editing is where business writing becomes good. A first draft is a hypothesis about how a piece could work. Editing is the experiment that tests that hypothesis and updates it.

## Editing is structural before it is verbal

Resist the temptation to start polishing sentences before checking the structure. A perfectly phrased sentence in the wrong paragraph is wasted work — you may delete it later anyway. Edit in passes, from large to small:

1. **Map vs. journey.** Does the piece you wrote match the piece you outlined? If not, decide whether the outline was wrong (update it) or the writing wandered (cut the wandering).
2. **Order.** Is each paragraph in the place that best serves the reader's understanding? Move paragraphs around before re-writing them.
3. **Paragraph integrity.** Does each paragraph make a single point? Split or merge as needed.
4. **Sentences.** Now you can shorten, sharpen, and recast.
5. **Words.** Replace jargon, hedges, and Latinate forms with plainer equivalents.
6. **Accuracy.** Fact-check names, numbers, dates, links, and any direct quotes.

A common mistake is to do passes 4–5 first, then realize at pass 1 that a section needs to be cut. That is wasted editing. Trust the order.

## Cut at least 10%

A first complete draft is almost always 10–30% longer than it needs to be. Setting an explicit target makes the cuts easier.

Concrete tactics:

- **Delete throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that…", "In today's fast-paced world…", "It can be argued that…". Almost always cuts cleanly.
- **Compress wordy connectives.** "In order to" → "to"; "due to the fact that" → "because"; "at this point in time" → "now."
- **Remove empty intensifiers.** very, really, truly, just, basically, literally, clearly. Most delete; the rest get replaced by a stronger verb.
- **Split fused sentences.** Two short sentences usually carry the same meaning more clearly than one long compound sentence — and end up shorter once split.
- **Remove duplicates.** Pieces often state the same point in two paragraphs, or the same example in two sentences. Keep the better version.
- **Trim adjectives.** A noun chosen well does not need three adjectives propping it up.

If you cannot cut 10%, the piece may be at the edge of underwritten. More commonly, you have not looked hard enough. Read it again with the target in mind.

## Read aloud — or simulate the reading aloud

Reading the draft aloud is the single most reliable editing test. It catches:

- Sentences that run out of breath.
- Unintentional rhymes and repeated sounds.
- Words that look fine on the page but do not belong in spoken English.
- Phrasing that the writer meant one way but the reader will hear another.

When reading aloud is impractical, slow down internal reading to roughly speaking pace. Most people can simulate the test with practice.

## Two kinds of second readers

When the piece matters, ask two kinds of people to read it before you finalize.

- **A domain expert.** Someone who already knows the topic. They catch mistakes of fact, missteps of interpretation, and arguments that experts will reject. They do not always represent the reader.
- **An intelligent generalist.** Someone close to the actual audience. They catch unexplained jargon, missing context, and logical leaps the writer made internally without writing down.

The generalist is more uncomfortable to ask, because asking implies "I think you might find this hard." Ask anyway. If a generalist reader trips on a paragraph, others will too — most will not tell you.

When the AI agent is producing the draft on the user's behalf, the user is by default the generalist reader. Make any unstated assumptions explicit before delivering.

## Editing other people's writing

Editing someone else's work is a different discipline. The temptation is to rewrite the piece into the version you would have written. Resist.

Two principles:

- **Comments before changes.** If a stylistic suggestion is debatable, leave a comment, not a re-write. Give the writer the chance to choose.
- **Light touch on direct edits.** When you do have authority to edit directly, fix what is clearly broken (typos, factual errors, broken structure) and leave the rest alone. The piece should still feel like the writer's voice afterwards.

The best editors leave a piece better than they found it, without leaving the writer feeling that the piece is no longer theirs. That feeling matters not just morally but practically: a writer who feels overwritten will write defensively next time.

When the AI agent is asked to edit someone else's prose, default to **suggestions and queries** rather than wholesale rewrites — unless the user explicitly asks for a full rewrite.

## The bold-and-fine-detail filter

Edward Carr's diagnostic, useful at a late editing pass: a piece works when it carries **bold statements** and **striking detail**, and discards the soft fabric in between.

- **Bold statements** are the few sentences that put your message across plainly: "Latency dropped 40% after the index migration." "We will not ship this in Q2."
- **Striking detail** is the anecdote, statistic, or vivid example that the reader will remember a week later.
- The middle layer — the cotton wool — is the prose that fills the space without doing work.

A useful editing pass: highlight the bold statements and the striking details. If the piece is mostly cotton wool between them, prune; if you cannot find any bold statements, the message is unclear; if you cannot find any striking detail, the piece will not be remembered.

## Final-pass checks

A small set of checks worth running on any piece before delivering:

- **Names and proper nouns.** Spelled correctly, used consistently throughout.
- **Numbers and dates.** Each one verified against its source.
- **Links.** Each one resolves; each one points where the surrounding sentence claims it does.
- **Acronyms.** Defined on first use unless universally familiar to the audience.
- **Title and headings.** They reflect the body. The title earns the open.
- **Opening and closing sentences.** They earn their position.
- **Tone match.** The register fits the audience and the channel.

## On the cost of small mistakes

A single typo, a misnamed person, or a wrong figure in an otherwise strong piece does disproportionate damage. The reader cannot distinguish a momentary slip from a sloppy thought process; both look the same on the page. Errors of detail also have a way of being the part the reader notices and remembers.

This is not a counsel of perfection — every writer ships errors — but a reminder that the final-pass check is not optional, and that the time spent on it is rarely the time you regret.

## Quality checks for the editing pass

- [ ] The piece has been edited from large to small (structure → paragraphs → sentences → words → accuracy).
- [ ] At least 10% of the words have been cut from the first complete draft.
- [ ] The piece has been read aloud, or at speaking pace.
- [ ] When stakes are high, both an expert and a generalist have reviewed.
- [ ] The bold statements and the striking details are clearly there.
- [ ] Names, numbers, dates and links have been verified.
